# NSGo Media architecture

NSGo Media is a Go module that NSGo apps import. A player reads a source with FFmpeg on its own workers. It decodes audio into the system's output and video into frames that a view draws at the display's pace. The app owns every control and reads the player's state as NSGo signals. The [roadmap](https://github.com/nsgo-dev/nsgo-media/issues/2), whose sub-issues are its stages, theirs the tasks by area and theirs the subtasks, orders what remains; the website shows it.

## Code map

| Package | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| `player` | The public API: a `Player` opened on a source, its commands, its state as signals, tracks and subtitle cues, and `View`, the component that draws its picture, with a `gpu` view on Windows, Linux and macOS for pictures on the GPU |
| `codec` | The public low-latency video codec API for other NSGo apps: encoding BGRA or NV12 frames to H.264 access units without B-frames, decoding access units to RGBA frames with hardware fallback, and probing the machine's available encoders and decoders |
| `internal/ffmpeg` | The binding of FFmpeg's C API through purego, checked against the library's version as it loads |
| `internal/ffmpeg/library` | Finding the FFmpeg libraries: an environment variable, the app's package, beside the executable, then the system's; on iOS the app's frameworks alone. Its `nsgo-natives.yaml` declares the published libraries and iOS frameworks, which `nsgo build` puts into an app, and the Linux packages they load |
| `internal/pipeline` | HTTP and HLS and DASH resources read in Go, choosing the variant, demuxing, bounded packet queues, decoders and their hardware fallback, the frame queue, hardware pictures shared with the views (D3D11 textures through `syscall`, VAAPI's DMA-BUFs through libva), the history for stepping backward, snapshots and the clock |
| `internal/audioout` | Audio output, one backend per target: CoreAudio on macOS, WASAPI on Windows, PulseAudio on Linux, whose package its `nsgo-natives.yaml` names, each with PCM and an IEC 61937 stream for passthrough; CoreAudio's AudioQueue for PCM on iOS, and what iOS does to the output under the app |
| `tools/ffmpeg` | The command that builds FFmpeg's LGPL shared libraries for each desktop target, and frameworks for iOS, at the pinned version, and writes the files of the release they are published in and the manifest declaring them |
| `internal/stats` | Counting the demo's and NSGo Player's use in the website's Umami, tagged `demo` and `app` beside the site's `website`: a pageview per page and heartbeats while in use, with a random install ID; `nsgo dev` builds and tests send nothing |
| `examples/demo` | A player app with its own controls, showing how an app drives the core |
| `app` | NSGo Player, the first-party desktop app on plugins, a Go module of its own ([NSGo Player](docs/app.md)) |
| `website` | The official site, Astro with Starlight, whose docs it reads from `docs/public/<language>/` and GitHub Pages serves at media.nsgo.dev |

## Boundaries

- Import only NSGo's public packages, never its `internal/`, so the core works with any NSGo release it supports. On iOS that leaves `AVAudioSession` to NSGo: the core holds it through `audio.HoldSession`, never sets its category or activates it itself, and only observes its notifications.
- Never use cgo or write C. FFmpeg, audio systems, Direct3D 11 and libva are reached through purego or `syscall`.
- Load only the FFmpeg major versions the binding names; refuse others instead of guessing struct layouts.
- Keep FFmpeg's GPL and nonfree components out of the libraries, so apps link the core under the LGPL.
- The reactive graph belongs to NSGo's engine goroutine. Workers reach it only through `Scope.Post`, and never block on it.
- Draw the picture through `core.Image`'s pixel buffers, or through a `gpu` view: on Windows and Linux one that imports a hardware picture, on macOS one that uploads an HDR picture's planes. App views drawn after it sit above it on every desktop. A native child window would cover them on Windows.
- Keep every queue bounded by packets, bytes and frames per player, so a slow consumer stops reading instead of growing memory.
- Make `Close` stop and join every worker before it returns. No callback or signal update follows it.
- Leave the app's look to the app: the core draws only the picture and ships no controls, strings or themes.

## Core flows

**Open.** `Open` starts a goroutine that opens the input, reads its streams and posts the source's information. Files go through FFmpeg's file protocol; HTTP(S) is read in Go (`httpSource`, ranged GETs sending the source's headers) through a custom `AVIOContext`. An HLS or DASH manifest, known by its media type or extension, opens FFmpeg's demuxer for it, whose playlists, segments and keys Go opens as well with the same headers ([adaptive streams](docs/ffmpeg.md#adaptive-streams)); each segment downloads ahead of the demuxer into at most 2 MiB, timed for the bandwidth estimate. An external audio source, as DASH serves one, is a second input on the same timeline with its own demuxer: its audio tracks follow the source's, its first is chosen, and it feeds only the audio queue. Each demuxer carries out every seek; the buffered range is the lesser of the inputs being read. Decoders for the chosen tracks start, video on the platform's hardware device first.

**Play.** The demuxer fills a packet queue per stream and waits while they hold 2 s and 25 packets each, or 48 MiB together. The audio decoder resamples to the device's format, slows or speeds it with `atempo`, and queues 300 ms for the device. Each device frame it fills is recorded against the media time it plays, so the device's played count is the master clock, eased between the device's updates; without audio a monotonic clock stands in. The video decoder copies hardware frames to memory, converts them to RGBA no larger than the largest view needs (tone-mapping HDR10 and HLG through ITU-R BT.2390 with BT.2020-to-BT.709 gamut conversion to sRGB), rotates them, and queues 3 in a pool of 7. On Windows and Linux, once every view's GPU device can import them, a hardware frame of NV12 or P010 stays on the GPU instead: D3D11VA's slice of its texture array is copied on the GPU into the pool frame's texture, shared through an NT handle with a keyed mutex, and a VAAPI surface is exported as a DMA-BUF a plane with libva. Its decoder's pool keeps 7 more surfaces for them. On macOS, once every view's GPU device takes them, an HDR10 or HLG frame keeps its planes in memory for the views to upload instead of converting to RGBA: VideoToolbox's P010 as copied, software decoding's converted to P010 by swscale; SDR frames convert as before. When NSGo imports VideoToolbox's surfaces ([#40](https://github.com/nsgo-dev/nsgo-media/issues/40)), they are meant to go to the views as another kind in place of the upload, as D3D11VA's and VAAPI's do. Each frame keeps its decoded picture, which a snapshot converts again at the source's size, copying a hardware one to memory first (and tone-mapping HDR frames the same way).

**Present.** At every `RequestAnimationFrame` the player runs the presenter: it starts the clock once the first frame and 100 ms of sound are ready, shows the newest frame due by the clock (a few milliseconds early, a lead that shifts when two frames fall due in one display frame) and drops late ones. It sets the frame into the view's image source with a new version; the frame shown before goes back to the pool a display frame later, after the commit that copied it ran. A frame on the GPU leaves the image empty: the `gpu` view under it requests `gpu.ColorSpaceExtendedLinearSRGB` when the display reports HDR active (`Display().HDR`) and `gpu.ColorSpaceDefault` otherwise, imports the frame's luma and chroma planes (on macOS, writes them from memory into two textures it keeps, a 10-bit sample as its two bytes, since NSGo creates no 16-bit textures, which the shader joins again), and converts them to RGB in a shader by the frame's matrix and range, turned as the stream is. On an HDR extended linear sRGB surface, HDR10 (PQ) and HLG output unclamped linear light (`1.0` = 203-nit SDR white) in Rec. 709 primaries and SDR pictures linearize to `[0, 1]`; on an SDR surface, HDR10 and HLG tone-map through ITU-R BT.2390 with BT.2020-to-BT.709 gamut conversion to sRGB while SDR passes through unchanged. It holds the frame until the fence of the last display frame that sampled it. Frames in memory, as software decoding and stepping backward give, show in the image above it.

**Subtitles.** The demuxer decodes subtitle packets as it reads them: every text track's into cues kept for the source, and only the chosen bitmap track's into RGBA bitmaps, turned as the picture is, kept within 30 s of the position and 64 MiB. A bitmap cue ends where the next of its track starts; one whose end is not read yet, as PGS', shows only within the span read without a break since it, so a seek past that span never shows it. A VobSub file's packets stay in memory and decode as they show.

**Variants.** An HLS or DASH stream's variants are its video tracks with a bandwidth. Unless the app chose one, every 2 s of playing the highest that fits 80% of the estimated bandwidth is weighed: a lower one is taken at once, a higher one 10 s after the last change. Changing the variant discards the other tracks, plays the variant's own audio when its audio is muxed with it, and seeks exactly to the position, where the decoders reopen for the new tracks. A live stream that cannot seek starts the new variant where the demuxer reads.

**Passthrough.** How an audio track plays is decided at its first packet after it opens or is chosen again, and again when the rate leaves or reaches 1× or passthrough changes, which start its sound again from the position. A track of a codec passthrough is on for, at 1×, and for DTS with no extension substream or core extension in its packet or profile, closes the PCM device and opens one for its IEC 61937 stream: WASAPI's exclusive mode with the codec's format, a CoreAudio device hogged with its mixing off and an encoded physical format (only digital outputs list one, so PCM-only outputs such as HDMI ports refuse), or a PulseAudio stream of the passthrough encoding through the asynchronous API, which PipeWire's server answers too. DTS-HD (a core followed by an extension substream, or a High Resolution or Master Audio profile) and TrueHD take HDMI's high bit rate instead, 8 channels at 192 kHz (176.4 kHz for 44.1 kHz's family): WASAPI's DTS_HD and DOLBY_MLP formats, an encoded CoreAudio format with 8 channels at that rate, or PulseAudio 13's TrueHD and DTS-HD encodings. Where it fails, DTS-HD tries its core's stream when DTS passes, then PCM opens again, and the first error is kept. FFmpeg's spdif muxer wraps each packet: E-AC-3 gathers six blocks a burst, TrueHD 24 access units a MAT frame, and DTS-HD's whole frames go as DTS type IV bursts at a `dtshd_rate` of 768 kHz; a burst is one chunk of the ring at the carrier's rate and channels, so the device's played count stays the clock. The ring sends a burst whole even across a seek, and pause bursts in every gap; muted or at no volume, a burst's time passes in pause bursts. The device keeps running while paused, sending pause bursts, so the receiver stays locked.

**iOS.** The libraries are frameworks in the app, and PCM plays through the AudioQueue macOS uses, whose timeline an interruption starts again from zero, so `Played` adds the timelines up. The player watches the session's notifications: an interruption beginning pauses it and its end plays it again when the system says it should resume and the interruption made the pause; an old device becoming unavailable, as headphones unplugged, pauses it until the app plays. There is no passthrough. While the app wants a player playing, from `Play` to a pause, the end, a failure or `Close`, it holds the session with `audio.HoldSession(core.AudioSessionPlayback)` before its output starts, so the session is NSGo's Playback level, which the Ring/Silent switch does not silence and which pauses other apps' audio; NSGo restores and deactivates it after the last holder. When the app's lifecycle goes to the background, a player pauses unless the app's Info.plist lists the `audio` background mode and its source has sound. Then it plays on there: no display frames come, so a 100 ms timer runs the presenter, and the pipeline's audio-only mode drops the picture's packets and decoded frames undecoded and lets the clock start and end without them. Coming back seeks exactly to the position, which decodes the picture again.

**Seek.** A seek raises the pipeline's generation, flushes the queues and holds the clock at the target. Packets, frames and samples of an older generation are dropped wherever they are met, so a stale frame never shows. An exact seek drops what decodes before the target; a fast one starts at the keyframe. A hardware decoder that fails gives way to software and seeks again to the shown position.

**Step.** While paused, a step forward shows the next queued frame. A step backward shows the previous frame from the history: decoded pictures, in memory, of an unbroken run of frames before the shown one, at most 48 frames or 128 MiB. On a miss the video decoder seeks to a window before the shown frame, a few frames by the average rate, and keeps the frames from it up to the shown one; a window that held none is read again twice as long, and one reaching the source's start ends stepping there. Frames are chosen by their decoded timestamps, so variable rates step exactly. Steps asked for before the last ones showed add up. Resuming after a step backward seeks exactly to the frame shown, so sound and picture start there; seeking and resuming clear the history.

## Data and state

The core persists nothing. A player's state lives in memory until `Close`; the app stores positions, history or settings itself.

## Verification

Pipeline and player tests decode the clips in `testdata/` headless, through a silent output paced as a device, and check order, seeking, steps both ways at constant and variable rates, snapshots, rate, looping, text and bitmap subtitles (their times, clears, seeking past an unread end, and placement), HTTP with its headers, external audio in sync and `Close`. HLS and DASH fixtures, served by a local HTTP server, check seeking, a live sliding window, changing the variant, byte ranges, AES-128 and `Close` cancelling a segment's request; DASH's are skipped with libraries that lack it. `internal/audioout`'s test plays a tone on the real device and opens it for AC-3 passthrough. Passthrough tests send `passthrough.mkv`'s AC-3, E-AC-3 and DTS tracks and `hbr.mkv`'s DTS-HD Master Audio and TrueHD to a mock endpoint and read its stream back as bursts, through muting, seeking, the rate, changing the track and `Close`, and check every fallback; the packer's tests check DTS type IV bursts and MAT frames, their codes and the access units through them. The fixtures' DTS-HD is a real core with an extension substream of padding, and their TrueHD FFmpeg's experimental encoder's, with no Atmos. On real devices (2026-11-22), WASAPI's exclusive mode took AC-3 and DTS on a Windows 11 PC's S/PDIF output and refused E-AC-3, DTS-HD and TrueHD, DTS-HD then passing its core; the built-in output on macOS refused AC-3 and the high bit rate; an HDMI sink of PipeWire 1.6 without passthrough formats refused them all, and with AC-3 and DTS set took both and refused the high bit rate, DTS-HD passing its core; each refusal played PCM in sync. No receiver has decoded the bursts yet, nor has a CoreAudio encoded format, E-AC-3's 192 kHz carrier or a device taking the high bit rate been tried. On the iOS simulator, `go tool nsgo test -target ios_simulator -env NSGO_MEDIA_FFMPEG=<unpacked ios_simulator archive> -env NSGO_MEDIA_TESTDATA=$PWD/testdata` runs the `audioout`, `ffmpeg`, `pipeline` and `player` tests, DASH among them, with VideoToolbox decoding; there a playing player sets the session's category to Playback and gives back the one from before after its pause. On the simulator (2026-11-24) the demo, which declares the `audio` background mode, played a 2-minute clip on through 17 s in the background and showed the picture at the position as it came back; without the mode it paused there. On a desktop, `go tool nsgo test -target macos_arm64 -env NSGO_MEDIA_TESTDATA=$PWD/testdata` runs them, DASH among them, with the libraries `nsgo-natives.yaml` declares. `nsgo build` with installers gave universal NSGo Player and demo apps and disk images holding both architectures' libraries, signed ad hoc, and the demo from its disk image loaded them from its Frameworks as it opened a clip (2026-11-24); a Developer ID signature and notarization are untried. The demo's tests run headless, one as a phone, since a playing picture keeps a target's steps from settling. On an iPhone (2026-11-22) the demo played a local file and an HLS stream on VideoToolbox, with an AudioQueue's clock, seeking, steps both ways and subtitles, and paused as the phone locked. On an iPhone (2026-11-24) the demo, with the `audio` background mode, played on in silent mode, in the background and with the phone locked, showed the picture at the position as it came back, paused as headphones were unplugged and as a call began, and played again as the call ended. On a Windows 11 PC (D3D11VA) and on Omarchy (VAAPI) (2026-11-25), hardware pictures of 8-bit H.264 and HEVC and 10-bit HEVC drew through the `gpu` view, under a child of the view and a view after it, and snapshots, steps both ways and software decoding gave what they gave before; `hdr10.mkv` (PQ) and `hlg.mkv` (HLG) played with hardware and software decoding and produced tone-mapped pictures and snapshots on both devices. On a MacBook Pro (M1 Max) with its XDR display (2026-11-25), 4K HDR10 and HLG clips played with VideoToolbox and software decoding on an extended linear sRGB surface under the demo's controls, and on a view made to request an SDR surface gave, within 1 of 255, the tone-mapped pixels of the RGBA path; an external SDR display is untried. `nsgotest` drives the demo app. `TestMeasurePlayback` plays a long clip on a real machine; the results are in [Performance](docs/performance.md). `codec` tests probe available encoders and decoders, round-trip BGRA and NV12 frames through H.264 hardware and software encoders and decoders, check forced keyframes, dropped-frame recovery and live bit-rate changes, and measure 1080p NV12 per-frame encode latency on macOS (VideoToolbox), Windows 11 (NVENC, QSV via libvpl, Media Foundation) and Omarchy (VAAPI, OpenH264). On the iOS simulator (2026-11-25), `Decoders` reported VideoToolbox and a decoder gave its pictures; the simulator's VideoToolbox opens no H.264 encoder, and encoding on an iPhone is untried.
